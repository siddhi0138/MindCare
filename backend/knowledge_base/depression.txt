Understanding Depression

Depression is more than temporary sadness. It is a persistent low mood, lasting two weeks or longer, that affects how a person thinks, feels, and functions day to day. Common signs include a loss of interest or pleasure in activities once enjoyed, persistent fatigue, changes in sleep or appetite, difficulty concentrating, feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt, and in some cases thoughts of death or self-harm.

Depression can look different for different people. Some experience it as sadness or emptiness, others as irritability, numbness, or physical symptoms like unexplained aches. It is not a sign of weakness or a character flaw — it is a common and treatable condition influenced by biological, psychological, and environmental factors.

Small, manageable steps that can help:
- Behavioral activation: scheduling small, achievable activities (a short walk, a shower, one chore) even when motivation is low, since motivation often follows action rather than preceding it.
- Maintaining a basic routine for sleep, meals, and movement, since depression often disrupts these first.
- Connecting with even one supportive person, briefly, rather than withdrawing completely.
- Journaling to track mood patterns and notice small improvements over time.
- Limiting alcohol, which is a depressant and can worsen symptoms.

Professional support matters. If low mood persists most of the day, most days, for two weeks or more, or is affecting work, relationships, or safety, it is important to talk to a licensed mental health professional. Effective treatments include therapy (such as CBT) and, when appropriate, medication prescribed and monitored by a qualified provider.
