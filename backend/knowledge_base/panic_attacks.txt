What Are Panic Attacks?

A panic attack is a sudden episode of intense fear or discomfort that peaks within about 10 minutes. It can happen without an obvious trigger, even during rest or sleep. Common physical symptoms include a racing or pounding heart, chest tightness, shortness of breath, trembling, sweating, dizziness, nausea, and a feeling of choking. Psychologically, people often report a fear of losing control, a fear of dying, or a sense of unreality (derealization) or detachment from themselves (depersonalization).

Panic attacks are the body's fight-or-flight response activating when there is no real physical danger. The symptoms, while frightening, are not medically dangerous on their own.

Common triggers include high stress periods, sleep deprivation, caffeine or stimulant use, and in some cases no identifiable trigger at all. Some people experience panic attacks as part of panic disorder, where repeated attacks lead to persistent worry about having more attacks.

In-the-moment coping techniques:
- Slow, controlled breathing (e.g., inhale 4 seconds, hold 4 seconds, exhale 6 seconds) helps counteract hyperventilation.
- Grounding techniques, such as the 5-4-3-2-1 method (naming 5 things you see, 4 you can touch, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, 1 you can taste), redirect attention to the present moment.
- Reminding yourself that the sensations, though intense, are temporary and not dangerous, can reduce the fear-of-fear cycle.
- Cold water on the face or holding an ice cube can activate the body's dive reflex and help calm the nervous system.

If panic attacks are frequent, disruptive to daily life, or accompanied by ongoing dread of future attacks, it is worth speaking with a licensed mental health professional, who can discuss options like cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which has strong evidence for treating panic disorder.
